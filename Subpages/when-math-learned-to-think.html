When Math Learned to Think

Exploring the mathematics behind artificial intelligence, reasoning and problem-solving.

Can mathematics help us describe something that looks like thinking? “When Math Learned to Think” explored the mathematical ideas that make it possible to frame problems, learn from examples, and reason about intelligent systems.

Opening moments of the mathematics event.

From a question to a mathematical model

Many problems begin in ordinary language: How do we find a route? How do we recognize a pattern? How do we make a decision when information is incomplete? Mathematics helps turn these questions into models with clearly defined assumptions, quantities, and goals.

The central question: What does it mean to translate an apparently intelligent action into steps that can be described, tested, and improved?

Introducing the Undergraduate Seminar and the event.

Representing a problem

Before a computer can search for a solution, we need a way to represent possible states and allowed actions. A maze is a useful example: positions become states, movements become connections, and finding an exit becomes a search problem.

The speaker discussing mathematical ideas connected with artificial intelligence.

Search, choices, and constraints

Different strategies explore possibilities in different orders. Some prioritize nearby options, while others use estimates to guide the search. Mathematics gives us a language for comparing these strategies and understanding their limitations.

A maze-solving example used during the talk.

What changes when a system learns?

Instead of writing a rule for every possible situation, learning systems use data to adjust parameters. Optimization helps select parameters that perform well on a chosen objective, while probability and statistics help us think about uncertainty and whether a result will generalize.

Understanding the limits

Solving a mathematical task is not automatically the same as human understanding. A model can be impressive in one setting and unreliable in another. This distinction makes careful problem definitions and evaluation essential.

Audience members following the talk.

Continuing the conversation

The event connected abstract mathematics with a familiar modern question: how can a sequence of formal operations produce behavior that appears intelligent? It is an invitation to explore the underlying ideas rather than treating intelligent technology as a black box.
